## Why this niche wins for us
1. **High job values, easy anchor.** A repair is a few hundred dollars. A system
   replacement is thousands. When the owner tells you what an average job is
   worth, the site pays for itself in one or two jobs and the math is instant.
   No other niche anchors this cleanly.
2. **Emergency demand they cannot afford to miss.** When a furnace dies at 11pm
   in January or an AC quits in a July heat wave, the customer calls whoever
   shows up first and looks trustworthy. If the site does not load fast, show a
   phone number, and say "24/7 emergency service," the next contractor gets the
   call. That urgency is the whole pitch.
3. **They live on Google and reviews.** Nobody has a brand-loyalty relationship
   with their HVAC company until they need one. The customer searches "AC repair
   near me," calls two or three, and picks on trust signals: reviews, a real
   site, a fast response. Owning that Google presence is owning the emergency
   call.
4. **Owner-operators who respect straight talk.** This buyer hates being sold to
   and respects someone who talks like a contractor and prices in jobs. Once you
   speak their language, you are not "a marketer," you are the person who makes
   the phone ring. That trust closes fast.

## Who signs the check
The owner-operator. Phone in a truck, hates meetings, respects trades, allergic
to marketing jargon. Sometimes an office manager or spouse runs the books and the
scheduling and has real say. Talk in jobs, not dollars. Talk in "the phone rings
twice more a week," not "conversion rate." Keep the call to 15 minutes and mean
it. Decision cycles are fast when the pain is a slow phone or a competitor
eating the emergency calls.

## What their site must do (our build spec, in priority order)
1. **Phone number everywhere, tap-to-call on mobile.** The emergency customer is
   on a phone and wants to call now, not fill a form. Number in the header,
   sticky on mobile, one tap. This is the single highest-value element.
2. **"Emergency service" and hours, above the fold.** 24/7 or same-day, stated
   plainly at the top. The customer with a dead furnace is scanning for exactly
   those words. If they are buried, the customer bounces to whoever says it
   first.
3. **Fast load, especially on mobile.** A slow site loses the emergency call
   before it renders. Speed is not a nice-to-have here, it is the conversion.
4. **Trust signals up front.** Licensed, insured, years in business, service
   area, and reviews streamed in. The customer is letting a stranger into their
   home. Show why to trust this one.
5. **Services listed plainly** (AC repair, furnace repair, install, maintenance,
   and for the trade, panels/water heaters/drains) so the search matches the
   page. And the service-area towns named, for local search and to self-qualify
   out-of-area calls.
6. **Book or request service, for the ones who take appointments.** Maintenance
   and non-emergency work can book online. Embed what they run (ServiceTitan,
   Housecall Pro, Jobber, or a simple request form), never replace it.
7. **A maintenance-plan page** if they sell one. Recurring service agreements are
   their best margin and deserve their own page, not a footnote.

## Seasonality calendar (this is the real spine of HVAC)
HVAC runs on two hard seasonal turns. This is a checkable calendar pattern, not a
guess, and it drives both outreach timing and care-plan content.
- **Spring (Mar-May): the AC push.** First warm days send everyone to test the
  AC and discover it is dead. Peak repair and pre-season tune-up demand. A site
  pushing spring AC maintenance ahead of the first heat wave catches the smart
  customer before the emergency rush.
- **Summer (Jun-Aug): emergency AC season.** Heat waves drive emergency calls.
  This is when "24/7 emergency AC repair" above the fold earns its keep. Highest
  emergency-demand window.
- **Fall (Sep-Nov): the furnace push.** First cold snap, everyone tests the
  heat and finds a problem. Pre-season furnace tune-ups and safety checks.
- **Winter (Dec-Feb): emergency heat season.** A furnace out in a cold snap is
  an emergency and a safety issue. "24/7 emergency heat" is the January headline.
- **Storm and heat-event spikes.** Major weather (heat waves, deep freezes,
  storms with outages) compounds failures and drives surge demand. Contractors in
  storm-prone metros feel this hard. A site that loads fast and screams emergency
  service wins the surge.

Use the calendar in outreach: pitch the AC book in early spring, the furnace book
in early fall, before the rush, not during it.

## The "show up first when their AC dies" angle (the core pitch)
This is the sentence that lands with an HVAC owner. Work it.
- **The scenario, out loud:** "It is 11pm in July, someone's AC just quit, and
  their house is 85 degrees. They grab their phone and search 'AC repair near
  me.' They call the first two that load fast, look legit, and say 24/7. Is that
  you, or the guy down the road?"
- **The fault, made concrete:** if their site loads slow, hides the phone
  number, or does not say emergency service, they are not in that customer's
  first two calls. The competitor with the faster site gets the biggest-margin
  call of the night.
- **The fix, in one line:** "I make you the first call. Fast site, phone number
  they can tap, emergency service and reviews right at the top. When their AC
  dies, you show up first and you look like the safe choice."

## Their objections (expected) and our counters
Price objections hit hardest here because job values are concrete and easy to
anchor. Straight from the objection bank, adapted.
1. "That's too expensive." -> "Compared to what? One missed job a month costs
   you more than this site does. What is an average job worth for you?"
2. "I can get a site for [REFERRAL_FEE] on Fiverr." -> "You can. You will get a template
   with your name on it. This is built off what is actually costing you calls,
   the faults I showed you. A template cannot see your slow load losing the 11pm
   AC call."
3. "Your competitor quoted me $600." -> "Take it if you trust it. Ask them to
   walk you through five specific faults on your current site first. That is the
   difference you are paying for."
4. "I get all my work from word of mouth and repeat customers." -> "Great, keep
   them. But a new-mover or an emergency at 11pm does not have your number, they
   have Google. A dead or slow site means that job goes to the guy who shows up
   first online. Word of mouth does not catch the emergency call."
5. "I am too busy to deal with a website." -> "You deal with it twice: 20
   minutes to hand me what you have, 20 minutes on day 3 to approve. I run the
   rest. Standard is live in 7 days." (busy owners buy handled)
6. "I am already booked solid." -> "Booked with your best-margin work, or
   whatever comes in? A site that ranks and catches the emergency and
   replacement calls fills the high-value calendar, not just the busy one."
7. Price -> anchor to their average job value, then the one-missed-job payback.

## The call script (ICP A: local service owner)
Use sales-calls.md ICP A verbatim, it was written for exactly this owner. The
three-question dig is the frame:
1. "What is an average job worth to you?" (sets the anchor for everything)
2. "Where do customers come from now, and what happens when they hit your site?"
3. "If the phone rang twice more a week, what would that change?"
Then walk one teardown fault (Stage 3), price in jobs with silence after (Stage
4), and close on "want it handled?" (Stage 5). Numbers out of their mouth, said
back to them, close harder than any pitch line.

## The wedge questions (discovery, this niche)
1. "What is an average job worth to you, and what is a full system replacement
   worth?"
2. "When someone's AC or furnace dies at night, how do they find you? What do
   they see?"
3. "How fast does your site load on a phone, and is your number one tap away?"
4. "How much of your work is emergency versus scheduled, and where do you lose
   the emergency calls?"
