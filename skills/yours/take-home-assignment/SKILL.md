# take-home-assignment

## When to use
A hiring process handed [OWNER] a take-home: an audit, a strategy doc, a mock campaign, a
"how would you approach X" case. Input: the assignment prompt and the company. Output: a
scope plan, a time box, the actual deliverable's spine, and the framing note that goes with
it. The postmortem lesson applies here too, he over-invests and burns out. This skill's job
is a submission that reads senior in a fixed number of hours.

## The trap and the rule
Take-homes are a judgment test disguised as a work test. The candidate who spends 20 hours
signals desperation and poor prioritization. The candidate who spends 3 hours on the right
3 things and says so signals an operator who ships. **Over-deliver on insight, signal on
volume.** Do the highest-impact 20% fully, gesture at the rest with "here's what I'd do
next with access to your data," and stop. Naming the time box in the note is itself the
strongest signal.

## Step 1 — scope it (what are they actually testing?)
Read the prompt for the real question behind it. "Audit our funnel" is not asking for a
50-page teardown, it's asking: can this person find the one thing that's leaking money and
say it plainly. Pick the 2-3 things that, if he nails them, make the hire obvious. Ignore
the rest on purpose. Write those down before touching the work.

## Step 2 — time-box it (protect the hours)
Set a hard cap: most take-homes deserve 2-4 hours, a senior strategy case maybe 5. Put the
cap in writing at the top of his working notes. When the cap hits, he ships what he has with
a "what I'd do next" section. A tight, finished 3-hour submission beats a sprawling 12-hour
one every time, and it protects him from the burnout pattern.

## Step 3 — over-deliver vs signal (the line, per section)
- **Over-deliver** on: the diagnosis (find the real leak), one worked recommendation with
  the reasoning, and one number that shows he thinks in dollars. This is where senior shows.
- **Signal and stop** on: exhaustive audits, polished decks, building the thing they can
  build once hired, anything that's free consulting. List it as "next steps with your data"
  instead of doing it on spec.

## Step 4 — the framing note (ships with the deliverable)
A short cover, 4-6 sentences, that frames the work as an operator's judgment, not a
homework dump. It states: the time he gave it (on purpose), the 2-3 things he chose to go
deep on and why, and one line on what he'd do first with real access. This note is often
what gets read most carefully. It shows he prioritizes, respects his own time, and thinks
about their business, not about impressing a grader.

## Hard lines
- **Never fabricate data.** If he's guessing at their metrics, say "assuming X, here's the
  math; with your real numbers I'd confirm." Invented specifics get caught and kill trust.
- **Don't build the actual thing free.** Show he can, don't do the job before the offer.
- **The number always appears.** At least one recommendation carries a dollar or a
  conversion-rate impact. Operators quantify.

## Voice (hard rules)
No em-dashes or en-dashes, ever. Short sentences, 9-13 words. Contractions always. The
framing note reads like an operator explaining his choices, not a student justifying them.
Confident, dry, no apology for the time box. Full spec:
`business-library/VOICE-SPEC.md`.

## Output structure (produce this)
1. **Scope read:** the real question behind the prompt, in one line.
2. **Time box:** the hours cap, stated.
3. **Go-deep list:** the 2-3 things he'll nail, and what he's deliberately skipping.
4. **Deliverable spine:** the structure of the submission (headers + the one number).
5. **The framing note:** the cover, ready to paste, 4-6 sentences.

## WORKED EXAMPLE — "Audit our funnel" case for a Series B SaaS
*Input: assignment reads "Take a look at our marketing funnel [link to their site + a
public pricing page] and tell us what you'd change." No data provided. Growth role.*

**Scope read:** they're testing whether he can find the one leak that matters and speak in
revenue, not whether he can list 40 CRO tweaks.

**Time box:** 3 hours. Stated at the top of the note so they know the judgment was
deliberate.

**Go-deep list:** (1) the top-of-funnel-to-trial handoff, where most SaaS leaks; (2) the
pricing page as a conversion surface; (3) one lifecycle gap. Deliberately skipping: full
technical SEO, ad-account teardown, brand critique. Those need their data and their
access, and doing them on spec is free consulting.

**Deliverable spine:**
- *The one leak:* the trial CTA is below the fold and competing with three other buttons.
  Name it, show where it is.
- *The fix + the math:* "If trial-start sits at even a conservative 2% of sessions and this
  moves it to 3%, at your traffic that's roughly N more trials a month. That's the whole
  point of the change." (Flag the assumption: "using a conservative estimate, your real
  numbers would sharpen this.")
- *One lifecycle note:* no visible nurture between trial and paid. One recommendation.
- *Next steps with your data:* a short list of what he'd pull first with real access
  (activation rate, trial-to-paid, channel mix). This is the "signal, don't build" section.

**The framing note (ships with it):**

> I gave this about three hours on purpose. With a take-home the useful signal isn't how
> much I can produce, it's what I'd prioritize on day one, so I went deep on the two or
> three things that move revenue and left the rest as next steps.
>
> The short version: your biggest leak is the trial handoff, not the top of the funnel.
> The CTA is buried and competing with itself. I've sketched the fix and a rough dollar
> estimate inside, flagged where I'm assuming numbers I'd confirm with your data.
>
> First thing I'd do with real access is pull activation and trial-to-paid, because that's
> where the money either compounds or leaks. Happy to walk through any of it.
>
> [OWNER]

*Why this works: it solves the real question (find the leak), quantifies one fix, refuses
to do the job for free, and the note frames the time box as judgment. Reads senior in three
hours, not desperate in twelve.*
